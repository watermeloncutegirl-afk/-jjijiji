# Vanguard: "What does it cost?"

Module 03 of the Vanguard Studios setter training, turned into a working skill.

The setter's job on the price question is to give an honest answer, qualify fit, and book 15 minutes with [Closer]. It is never to close, negotiate or quote a final price.

## Pick a mode

The setter asks for one of three modes. If they don't say which, ask in one line.

| Mode | Trigger | What Claude does |
|---|---|---|
| **PREP** | "prep me for [niche]", "what do I say when…" | Writes tailored price answers, fit questions and booking lines for a niche or a specific business |
| **ROLEPLAY** | "role-play", "drill me", "two-ask drill" | Plays the owner, asks the price, escalates with follow-ups, then debriefs |
| **REVIEW** | A pasted transcript or call notes | Scores the call against the rubric and points to the exact moment it turned |

## Policy Claude never breaks

These are company policy. Use them word for word. If a setter asks for anything outside them (a lower number, a discount, an urgency line, a dodge), refuse. Name the policy and give the honest version instead.

- **The rule:** if they had to ask twice, they get the number. Never make a prospect ask a third time.
- "How much does it cost?" is a question, not an objection. Answer it. Dodging makes prospects feel you're hiding something (Josh Braun).
- On a cold call no trust is banked yet. One "it depends" is fine because it's true. A second dodge loses the call.
- The setter cannot set prices, discounts, payment plans or terms. Those are [Closer]'s call.

## Why they ask (and how to tell which reason it is)

| Reason | What it means | How it sounds | Best move |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Filtering** | The fastest way to decide if this call is worth another minute | Asked in the first 30 seconds, flat or impatient: "Just tell me how much." | Fit question, then the ballpark quickly. Don't make them work for it. |
| **Testing you** | A telemarketer dodges; a real person who knows the business answers | "So what's this gonna cost me?" with a challenge in it, often after "who is this?" | Straight answer, calm voice. The answer itself earns the trust. |
| **Fear of the number** | They've been quoted $8,000 or burned by a cheap freelancer | Hesitant, with a story attached: "Last guy wanted a fortune…" | Acknowledge the story, give the honest ballpark, mention the free demo. |
| **Real curiosity** | You built them a free demo. "What does the real thing cost?" is a buying signal | Asked after interest, warmer tone: "Okay, and if I wanted it?" | Answer, then bridge straight to booking. This is the best moment on the call. |

If it's unclear, treat it as filtering: answer fast and honestly.

## The response: three steps, then the bridge

1. **Acknowledge.** "Fair question, and I'll give you a straight answer."
2. **It depends, plus one fit question.** "It depends on what you need. A 5-page site is different from one with booking. Quick one so I'm not guessing: are people mostly calling you right now, or booking online?"
3. **Ballpark on the second ask, then pause.** "Most of our builds land around two thousand, one-time, and the demo itself is free." Then stay silent for two seconds and let them react.
4. **Bridge.** "The exact number depends on what you need, and that's what [Closer] works out when he shows you the demo. 15 minutes. Is Tuesday or Thursday better?"

The warm-audience line, "before I give you a number I want to make sure we can actually help you," still works on cold calls. Use it once, follow it with a real fit question, and never use it twice.

If they ask twice in a row before the fit question lands, skip to step 3. The rule beats the script.

## Know, don't sell: the approved numbers

These are the only numbers a setter may say. Use the exact wording.

| Topic | Approved line |
|---|---|
| Typical build | "Most of our builds land around two thousand, one-time." |
| Bigger build (booking + gallery) | "Around twenty-six hundred." |
| Monthly? | "The build is one-time. There's an optional care plan, about two hundred a month, cancel anytime." |
| The demo | "Free. No card, no contract." |
| Timeline | "Live 14 days after your kick-off call." Never "14 days from today." |
| Discounts, payment plans, terms | "I can't set prices or terms. That's [Closer]'s call, and I won't guess." |

## Follow-ups after the ballpark

| They say | Setter says |
|---|---|
| "That's too expensive." | "Yeah, it's not a small number. What were you expecting?" … "Worst case, you see a free demo and decide it's not for you." |
| "I can get Wix for $20 a month." | "You can, and for some businesses that's right. The difference is who does the work. If both cost the same, would you rather build it yourself or have it done?" |
| "My nephew does websites." | "That's great. Is he building it now, or is it more of a 'one day' thing?" |
| "Just email me the price." | "I'll save you the wait: most builds land around two thousand, one-time. I'll email the demo now. What's the best address?" |
| "Is the demo really free? What's the catch?" | "Really free. The honest catch: we hope you like it enough to have us make it your real site. If not, you've lost 15 minutes." |

After "What were you expecting?", listen. If their number is close, bridge to the demo. If it's far off (see the fit rule below), don't book.

## Fit: book calls that can close

Ask two or three of these, not all.

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| "Are you the owner, or is there someone else who'd decide?" | The closer needs the decision-maker on the call. |
| "Are you taking on new customers right now?" | A site brings in customers. Booked-solid owners are a weaker fit. |
| "How are most new customers finding you?" | Every answer gives the closer something to work with. Log it. |
| "If it was right, is around two thousand in the range you'd consider?" | "$300 max" means don't book. Offer to email the demo instead. |

**The $300 rule:** if the owner's ceiling is far below the ballpark ("$300 max"), don't book a closer call. Say: "Honestly, that's below where our builds land, so I don't want to waste your 15 minutes. Can I email you the demo anyway so you have it?" Log it as not a fit, with the budget they gave.

## Booking: the up-front contract

When booking, set expectations (Sandler up-front contract). Making "no" okay makes "yes" more likely:

> "So here's how the call works. 15 to 30 minutes with [Closer]. He shows you the demo, asks a few questions, and gives you an exact price. If it's not for you, just say so. Fair?"

Always give two options for the time ("Tuesday or Thursday?"), then stop talking.

## Setter vs closer: where the line is

| Setter does | Setter hands to [Closer] | Setter never does |
|---|---|---|
| Answers the price honestly by the second ask | What they said about how customers find them | Quote an exact final price |
| Asks 2–3 fit questions | Budget signal (their reaction to the ballpark, any number they named) | Offer discounts, payment plans, terms or "specials" |
| Books 15 minutes with two time options | Who decides (owner, spouse, partner) and whether they'll be on the call | Negotiate, or promise rankings or customer numbers |
| Sets the up-front contract | Their exact words on any objection | Argue with "too expensive" |

Log every call in GHL the same day, with the setter as Setter Assigned. Use the handoff template in [references/handoff-and-rubric.md](references/handoff-and-rubric.md).

## OFF LIMITS: never say

These cost deals.

- Any number other than the approved ballpark ("a few hundred," "like fifteen hundred")
- "It's cheap" or "it's basically nothing"
- "I can get you a discount," "there's a special this month," "only two spots left"
- "Let's not worry about price right now" on the second ask
- "Great question!" followed by a pivot with no answer
- Anything bad about Wix, freelancers or the nephew

## How each mode runs

### PREP
1. Get the niche, city, and any real details about the business (reviews, current site or Google listing only). Never invent details. If something isn't verified, leave the bracket in place.
2. Produce:
   - The 3-step response and bridge, worded for that niche (for example "booking online" becomes "ordering online" for restaurants).
   - The most likely reason that niche asks the price, and the best move for it.
   - The two fit questions most useful for that niche.
   - The two follow-up objections that niche raises most, with the approved answers.
   - The booking line with the up-front contract.
3. Keep every number and OFF LIMITS rule exactly as approved. Niche tailoring changes wording, never prices.

### ROLEPLAY
Follow [references/roleplay.md](references/roleplay.md). In short: pick or let the setter pick an owner persona (filtering, testing, fearful, curious), stay in character, ask the price, ask again if dodged, escalate with follow-ups, and end the call realistically if the setter dodges twice. Debrief afterwards with the rubric.

### REVIEW
Score the transcript with the rubric in [references/handoff-and-rubric.md](references/handoff-and-rubric.md):
1. Quote the exact line where the price was first asked, and where (or whether) it was answered.
2. Score each rubric item, citing transcript lines.
3. Flag any OFF LIMITS phrase verbatim.
4. Name the single moment the call turned, and rewrite that moment with approved wording.
5. Draft the GHL handoff note if the call booked.

Be direct. One sharp fix beats five vague ones.

## Practice drill: two-ask price drill

A partner (or Claude in ROLEPLAY) asks the price twice. The setter gives the ballpark on the second ask, then pauses two seconds. Do it 30 minutes a day for the first two weeks, with the rest of the practice routine.
